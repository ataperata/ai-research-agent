# Does daylight saving time actually save energy?

_Mode: offline demo · 5 sources cited · 2 excluded · verification: 15/15 sentences supported_

## Key findings

- The 2008 Department of Energy report estimated that the four extra weeks of daylight saving time reduced total electricity use by about 0.5 percent per day during the extended period. [1]
- Kotchen and Grant found that daylight saving time increased residential electricity demand in Indiana by about 1 percent overall. The increase was largest in the fall, when it reached 2 to 4 percent. [2]
- A meta-analysis published in The Energy Journal in 2018 combined the results of 44 studies on daylight saving time and electricity use. [3]
- During the 1973 oil embargo, the United States briefly adopted year-round daylight saving time to save energy, but the experiment was dropped within a year after complaints about dark winter mornings. [4]

## Overview

Benjamin Franklin is often credited with inventing daylight saving time, but his 1784 letter to a Paris newspaper was satire. He joked that Parisians could save money on candles by getting out of bed earlier. [4] Energy savings have been the main official justification for daylight saving time for more than a century. [4] The size of the effect depends on geography: countries farther from the equator tended to save more electricity, while subtropical regions often used more electricity under daylight saving time. [3] The Energy Policy Act of 2005 extended daylight saving time in the United States by about four weeks, beginning in 2007. [1]

## Evidence from studies

The Indiana study estimated that daylight saving time cost households in the state about $9 million per year in higher electricity bills. [2] Studies of the 2000 Sydney Olympics, when two Australian states extended daylight saving time, found no drop in overall electricity consumption. [3] Congress asked the Department of Energy to study how the extension affected energy use. [1] Because Indiana's climate is typical of much of the United States, the authors argued that the findings likely apply beyond the state, although results may differ in other climates. [2]

## Measured effects

Across the 44 studies, the average effect of daylight saving time was a reduction in electricity consumption of about 0.34 percent while it was in force. [3] Across the full extension, the electricity savings added up to roughly 1.3 billion kilowatt-hours, about 0.03 percent of total annual U.S. electricity consumption. [1]

## Limits and counterpoints

Supporters of permanent daylight time point to more evening daylight for shopping and recreation, while energy savings now play a smaller role in the debate. [5]
