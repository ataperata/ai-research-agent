What Indiana Taught Us About Daylight Saving Time and Electricity

By Department of Economics Research Briefs

2011-11-15

For decades, most of Indiana's counties did not observe daylight saving time. In 2006 the entire state began to observe it, which created a rare natural experiment for researchers.

Economists Matthew Kotchen and Laura Grant used more than seven million monthly meter readings from households in southern Indiana to compare electricity use before and after the switch.

Kotchen and Grant found that daylight saving time increased residential electricity demand in Indiana by about 1 percent overall. The increase was largest in the fall, when it reached 2 to 4 percent.

The study explains the result as a trade-off. Daylight saving time reduces demand for evening lighting, but it raises demand for heating on dark, cool mornings and for air conditioning on warm afternoons and evenings.

The Indiana study estimated that daylight saving time cost households in the state about $9 million per year in higher electricity bills. They also estimated social costs from added pollution emissions of $1.7 million to $5.5 million per year.

Because Indiana's climate is typical of much of the United States, the authors argued that the findings likely apply beyond the state, although results may differ in other climates.

References

  1. Kotchen, M. J. and Grant, L. E. (2011). Does Daylight Saving Time Save Energy? Evidence from a Natural Experiment in Indiana. Review of Economics and Statistics, 93(4).
