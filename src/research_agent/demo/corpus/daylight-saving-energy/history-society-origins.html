Why We Change the Clocks: A Short History of Daylight Saving Time

By Ellen Marsh

2021-03-10

Benjamin Franklin is often credited with inventing daylight saving time, but his 1784 letter to a Paris newspaper was satire. He joked that Parisians could save money on candles by getting out of bed earlier.

Germany and Austria-Hungary were the first countries to adopt daylight saving time nationally, in 1916, as a way to conserve coal during World War I. Britain and several other European countries followed within weeks.

The United States first adopted daylight saving time in 1918, and the Uniform Time Act of 1966 later standardized when it starts and ends.

During the 1973 oil embargo, the United States briefly adopted year-round daylight saving time to save energy, but the experiment was dropped within a year after complaints about dark winter mornings.

Energy savings have been the main official justification for daylight saving time for more than a century. Electricity use has changed a great deal since then: lighting is now a smaller share of household energy use, while heating and air conditioning are larger.
