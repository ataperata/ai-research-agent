Summary: Impact of Extended Daylight Saving Time on National Energy Consumption

By Office of Energy Analysis

2008-10-15

The Energy Policy Act of 2005 extended daylight saving time in the United States by about four weeks, beginning in 2007. Clocks now move forward on the second Sunday in March and back on the first Sunday in November.

Congress asked the Department of Energy to study how the extension affected energy use. The resulting 2008 report to Congress compared electricity data from utilities across the country for the days before and after each clock change.

The 2008 Department of Energy report estimated that the four extra weeks of daylight saving time reduced total electricity use by about 0.5 percent per day during the extended period. Savings were largest in the evening hours, when lighting demand fell, while demand rose slightly in the morning.

Across the full extension, the electricity savings added up to roughly 1.3 billion kilowatt-hours, about 0.03 percent of total annual U.S. electricity consumption.

The Department of Energy cautioned that the effect was small compared with normal swings in electricity demand caused by weather and economic activity.

References

  1. U.S. Department of Energy, Impact of Extended Daylight Saving Time on National Energy Consumption, Report to Congress, October 2008.
