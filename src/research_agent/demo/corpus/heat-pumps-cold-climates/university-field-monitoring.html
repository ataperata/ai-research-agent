Field Monitoring of Cold-Climate Air-Source Heat Pumps

By Cold Climate Housing Research Group

2022-02-14

Heat pump efficiency falls as outdoor temperatures drop. There is less heat in colder air and the compressor has to work harder to raise it to indoor temperature, so both heating capacity and COP decline.

Older air-source heat pumps often struggled below about 25°F (-4°C), which is one reason many people still believe heat pumps do not work in cold climates.

Modern cold-climate models use variable-speed, or inverter-driven, compressors and improved refrigerant circuits. Many are rated to keep heating at outdoor temperatures of -13°F (-25°C) or lower.

Field studies of cold-climate units in northern U.S. states have generally found seasonal average COPs above 2, with COPs between roughly 1.5 and 2 on the coldest days near 0°F (-18°C).

In cold, humid weather, frost builds up on the outdoor coil. The heat pump periodically runs a short defrost cycle, which temporarily reduces the heat delivered to the home.

Because capacity falls as temperatures drop, sizing matters. A unit sized only for mild weather may need backup heat during the coldest hours of the year.
